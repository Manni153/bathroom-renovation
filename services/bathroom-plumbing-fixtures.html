Who This Is For

Five situations that usually bring people to this page

A shower valve that's failed or gone inconsistent

Your mixer shower or thermostatic valve has started running hot then cold without warning, drops to a dribble whenever a tap or toilet is used elsewhere in the property, or has simply stopped delivering hot water at all. Sometimes it's the valve itself wearing out — cartridges and thermostatic elements do have a working life — and sometimes it's a symptom of something further back in the supply, which is why we check before quoting a straight swap. What people are usually worried about is spending money on a new valve only to find the same problem returns a few months later because the real cause was never fixed. Once diagnosed and replaced properly, the fix is generally permanent and the shower goes back to feeling the way it did when the bathroom was new.

Swapping a bath, basin or vanity without a full renovation

You want a freestanding bath instead of the built-in one that's currently there, or a dated vanity and basin swapped for something more current, without redoing the tiling, the layout, or anything else around it. This is one of the most common standalone jobs we do — the room itself is fine, it's just the fixture that's let it down, and a full renovation would be a disproportionate answer to a fairly contained problem. The main things owners want reassurance on are whether the new piece will fit the existing plumbing connections without moving them, and whether the edges will be sealed properly so it doesn't become a leak risk later. Where the swap is genuinely straightforward — same footprint, same waste and supply points — it's usually a same-day or next-day job rather than something that disrupts the household for a week.

Weak or uneven mains pressure in an older property

Showers that never quite feel right no matter what's been replaced — a weak trickle, pressure that drops the moment someone runs a tap elsewhere, or a system that can't cope with a rainfall shower head — is a genuinely common complaint in older Algarve properties that run straight off mains pressure with no pump or accumulator tank anywhere in the system. It's easy to assume the valve or the shower head is at fault and replace it, only to find the same weak flow on the brand new fixture, because the real limitation was upstream all along. We treat this as a diagnostic question first: is it the fixture, the pipework, or the incoming supply pressure itself, particularly on an upper floor or at the end of a long run. In most cases a booster pump or accumulator tank resolves it without any need to re-pipe the property.

A WC that runs constantly or is slow to refill

A toilet cistern that keeps trickling water long after a flush, or takes an age to refill before it's usable again, is annoying and — over months — genuinely expensive on the water bill, but it's rarely anything more serious than a worn cistern mechanism. The fill valve, flush valve or seal inside the cistern wears out over years of ordinary use, and in most cases the fix is a straightforward mechanism replacement rather than a whole new WC. Owners sometimes assume a constantly-running toilet points to a bigger problem behind the wall, so part of the job is simply confirming that it doesn't and giving a clear, honest answer either way. Where the WC itself is old enough that parts are hard to source, or the porcelain is chipped or cracked, we'll say so and quote a full replacement instead of chasing an outdated part.

Upgrading taps and a shower valve to a matching finish

You've decided the room's fixtures no longer match — a mix of chrome and brushed finishes, or taps that were fine individually but never looked coordinated as a set — and you want the taps, shower valve and any exposed fittings replaced with a single matching finish and style, without touching the tiling or the layout at all. This is a cosmetic and functional upgrade rather than a repair, and it tends to have the fastest turnaround of anything on this page since there's rarely anything to diagnose. The main planning question is compatibility: matching new fittings to existing pipe centres and connection types so nothing needs to be chased into the wall to move a supply point. Done well, it's one of the highest-impact, lowest-disruption changes a bathroom can have — the room reads as considerably more current for a modest, contained piece of work.

What actually happens, stage by stage

A plumbing and fixture job is smaller than a renovation, but it still follows a proper sequence rather than a quick swap-and-go. Here's what that looks like in practice, and the questions that tend to come up along the way.

Diagnosis and assessment

Before anything is disconnected, we work out what's actually wrong rather than assuming the obvious answer is the right one. A shower that feels weak could be a worn valve cartridge, a partially blocked flow restrictor, scale build-up in older pipework, or genuinely low incoming pressure that has nothing to do with the fixture at all — and treating the wrong one means spending money without fixing the problem. Where pressure is a possible factor, we check flow rate and pressure at the outlet against what would be expected for the property's supply type before recommending a valve swap, a pump, or both. For a WC or a leak, the same logic applies: we confirm whether the issue is a worn part, a failed seal, or something further into the supply or waste run, and we explain what we've found before agreeing a scope.

Isolating and disconnecting the old fixture

Every job starts with isolating the water supply to the fixture being worked on — using existing isolating valves where they're fitted and functional, or the property's main stopcock where they're not, since old or seized isolating valves are common in properties that haven't had plumbing work done in years. The old fixture is then disconnected and removed: unscrewed and lifted clear for a tap or shower valve, or carefully freed from its sealant and fixings for a basin, WC or bath, taking care not to damage surrounding tiling in the process. This is also the point where a problem hiding behind or beneath the old fixture — a failed seal, a damp patch, corrosion on old pipework — tends to surface, which is why we don't treat removal as a formality to rush through.

Fitting a new shower valve or mixer

A new mixer shower or thermostatic valve is fitted to the existing supply pipework where the connections and pipe centres allow it, tested for temperature stability and pressure across its full range, and checked for leaks at every joint before the wall plate or trim is finished off and sealed. Thermostatic valves in particular need commissioning — setting the maximum temperature limit correctly — which we do as standard rather than leaving factory defaults in place. If the new valve's connections don't line up with what's already there, we say so at quote stage rather than discovering it mid-job and improvising a fix that compromises the finish.

Fitting a new WC, basin or vanity

A WC is reconnected to its existing waste and supply, refitted with a new sealed connection at the pan-to-soil-pipe joint, and checked for a secure, level, non-rocking fit before it's sealed to the floor. A basin or vanity is secured to the wall or floor with fixings rated for its weight — this matters more than it sounds, since a wall-hung basin needs either a solid fixing point or a support frame behind the plasterboard, and we check for that rather than assuming it's there. Every new fixture is sealed at its junctions with the wall or floor using a proper sanitary sealant, and the whole connection is run and checked for drips or slow leaks before we consider the job finished, not just switched on and walked away from.

Pressure diagnostics and pump or accumulator installation

Where low pressure is the underlying issue, we test flow rate and pressure at the fixture, trace it back through the accessible pipework, and confirm whether a booster pump or an accumulator tank is the appropriate fix for the property's setup — the two solve slightly different problems, and fitting the wrong one means the complaint comes back. Installation includes finding a suitable location (usually a nearby cupboard, utility space or loft void), running the necessary pipework and a power connection to it, and setting the unit up for the property's actual demand rather than an off-the-shelf default. Once fitted, we retest at the fixture to confirm the improvement is real and not marginal, since the whole point of the exercise is a shower that actually feels different afterward.

Cost Guidance

What plumbing and fixture work typically costs

Regional guide figures for the Algarve — your written quote will reflect the actual fixtures, access and pipework condition on site. What drives the price of a job like this is rarely the fixture itself — a mixer shower valve costs roughly the same to fit whether it's the only thing being replaced or one of several — it's the number of fixtures being worked on in a single visit, and whether we're supplying the fixture as well as fitting it. A single tap or valve swap with a client-supplied fixture is close to a pure labour charge, whereas supplying and fitting a full set of new sanitaryware involves sourcing, delivery coordination and the fixtures' own cost on top of the fitting time.

Access also plays a role: a ground-floor villa with easy pipework access is quicker to work on than an apartment where supply runs are boxed in or shared with neighbouring units, and that difference shows up in labour time rather than the fixtures themselves.

A Common Question

Fixture swap, or full renovation?

Not every plumbing complaint needs to turn into a bigger project, and not every bigger project should be avoided just because it started as a simple fixture question. A standalone plumbing and fixture job is the right scope when the fixture is genuinely the only issue — the tiling and waterproofing beneath and around it are sound, nothing is cracked or discoloured, and the room's layout already works for you. Most of the scenarios on this page fall into that category, which is why they're quick, contained jobs rather than open-ended ones.

It's worth considering the bigger scope instead when tiling is already going to be disturbed for another reason (fitting a new bath sometimes means removing tiles that can't be reused, for instance), when several unrelated things need attention at once and doing them separately would mean paying for site setup and protection more than once, or when a fixture swap uncovers a waterproofing problem serious enough that a spot repair isn't the right long-term answer — see our waterproofing page for how that's handled on its own. If your bathroom needs more than fixtures — new tiling, a different layout, or a genuine strip-out — our full bathroom renovation service covers that as one coordinated project. We'll tell you plainly during the site visit which category your job falls into, rather than defaulting to the larger, more expensive scope.
