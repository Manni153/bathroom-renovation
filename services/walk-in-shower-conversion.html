Who This Is For

Five situations that usually bring people to us

The bath that's never used

The bath sits at one end of the room exactly where it always has, but in practice it's become a shelf — folded towels, the laundry basket, maybe a stack of magazines nobody reads in the tub. Every clean of the room still has to work around it, and the two or three baths a year it actually sees don't come close to justifying the floor space it occupies or the extra tiling around it. What usually tips someone from thinking about this to booking it is doing the sums on how rarely the bath gets used against how much space and effort it demands every week. Once it's converted, that same footprint becomes a walk-in shower you'll use daily, with a screen that's far easier to keep clean than a shower curtain ever was.

An ageing shower tray with a step

The shower itself might still work fine, but the tray has a lip of ten or fifteen centimetres that used to be a non-issue and now needs real care to step over, especially with wet feet and an early-morning routine. It's rarely framed as an emergency — more a low-level worry that grows with each passing year, sometimes prompted by a parent's fall elsewhere, sometimes by a knee or hip that's started giving trouble. A low-threshold tray or a tiled-in level-entry base fitted within the same footprint removes the step entirely without re-falling the whole room's floor or moving the drain. Once it's in, the shower reads as a deliberate, current fitting rather than a retrofit, and the day-to-day risk that prompted the call is simply gone.

A hotel-style look before selling or a family visit

The current shower cubicle or bath does the job, but it reads as exactly what it is — a fitting from a decade or two ago that dates the rest of an otherwise reasonable bathroom. It's usually noticed rather than mentioned outright: a viewing that goes quiet in the bathroom, a relative's polite silence, a nagging sense that the room undersells the property. A glass-screened walk-in shower with a low-threshold base and clean tiling reads as current and well cared for in a way a dated bath or a tired cubicle simply doesn't, and it's a comparatively contained, fast job to get there next to a full renovation. For owners timing this around a sale, a valuation, or hosting family for an extended stay, that combination of speed and visible impact is usually exactly what they're after.

Downsizing now the kids are grown

For a family bathroom that's served its purpose for years, a bath that once got daily use for small children now sits mostly empty, and the room has quietly become dead space built around a fixture nobody needs anymore. What's usually behind the decision is straightforward: the household's habits have changed, but the bathroom hasn't caught up, and a bath that isn't used is awkward to justify keeping when the space could work harder as a larger, more comfortable shower. Converting it opens up the room without the cost or disruption of a full renovation — the layout, tiling scheme and fixtures elsewhere can generally stay as they are, with the shower conversion running as a contained, standalone project. The result is a bathroom that matches how the household actually lives now, rather than how it lived a decade ago.

A second or guest bathroom that only ever needs a shower

Not every bathroom in the property needs a bath, and a guest bathroom or ensuite that's rarely used for anything but a quick shower is often the one still carrying a bathtub nobody asked for. It's frequently the smallest or most awkwardly shaped bathroom in the house, which makes the bath feel even more like wasted space once you notice it. Converting this kind of bathroom is usually one of the more straightforward jobs we do, since guest and second bathrooms are less likely to need layout changes and are rarely in daily use during the work, which makes scheduling easier too. Once done, it reads as a smart, sized-right guest bathroom instead of a smaller, cheaper copy of the main one.

What's Included

Everything between strip-out and final fix

This is a lighter-touch conversion than a full wet room — it waterproofs and tiles the shower zone on a tray or tiled-in base, rather than re-falling the whole room's floor to a single drain. Here's what actually happens at each stage.

Removal and disposal of the old bath or shower cubicle

The old bath or shower cubicle is disconnected, broken down where necessary to get it out through the door, and removed from the property along with any tiling or flooring coming out with it. We protect the route from the bathroom to the front door with sheeting and boards before anything is carried through, since a heavy cast-iron or acrylic bath can mark walls and flooring on the way out if it isn't managed carefully. Everything removed is bagged, boxed or wrapped as appropriate and taken away for disposal through licensed waste channels — nothing is left in a skip on the street for weeks. If the old bath or cubicle is in decent condition and you'd rather it went to someone who wants it than to disposal, tell us in advance and we'll set it aside instead.

Waste and supply adjustment within the footprint

Most conversions keep the shower on the same waste and supply points the bath or old cubicle was already using, which is the faster and cheaper route and the one we recommend where it works for the room. Where the layout allows it, that might mean nothing more than repositioning a waste outlet a short distance to line up with a new tray or drain point; where it doesn't, moving the drain across the room or to a different wall is possible but adds time and cost, and in apartment buildings can mean working near a shared riser, which we'll flag at the site visit. We test the waste and supply run before it's tiled over and covered, not after, so any issue surfaces while it's still easy to put right.

Waterproofing the shower zone

This is the stage that decides whether the conversion lasts and whether it leaks, and it's not one we compress to save a day. The shower zone — tray upstand, walls to shower-head height, and the floor area around a tiled-in base — is waterproofed with a liquid-applied membrane system to NP EN 14891 practice, with particular attention to the tray-to-wall junction, internal corners and the drain collar, since those junctions are where a membrane typically fails first if it's rushed or poorly detailed. The membrane needs proper cure time before tiling starts, and we build that time into the schedule we give you rather than treating it as a flexible buffer to make up time elsewhere.

The tray or level-entry base

A low-threshold shower tray sits a few centimetres above the floor rather than the ten-or-more centimetres of an older tray, which is enough to remove the trip risk without needing to alter the floor structure beneath it — usually the faster and cheaper of the two base options. A tiled-in level-entry base goes further: the floor is built up and gently falled to a linear or point drain so the shower area sits flush with the rest of the room, with no step or lip at all. It takes more site work and a day or two longer, but it's the closer match to a genuine wet-room look within a shower-zone-only footprint. We talk through both against your floor's existing structure and your budget at the site visit, rather than defaulting to one.

The screen or enclosure

A fixed glass panel is the simplest option and works well where the shower sits in a corner or recess with only one open side to enclose. A pivot or hinged door suits a slightly wider opening and gives a fully sealed enclosure; a sliding door is worth considering where floor space in front of the shower is tight, since it doesn't need clearance to swing open. Frameless glass gives the cleanest look and is easier to keep visibly clear of seals, but costs more than a framed or semi-framed screen and needs a perfectly true wall and floor to sit flush — something we check at the site visit rather than assuming. Whichever option you choose, the glass is toughened safety glass as standard.

Tiling

Wall and floor tiles are set on a waterproof-compatible adhesive once the membrane has cured, with attention to correct falls to the drain and consistent grout lines, and any surrounding tiling disturbed by the old bath or cubicle's removal is made good to match. Porcelain is the most common choice for a shower zone given its low maintenance and water resistance, though large-format porcelain (fewer grout lines, a cleaner look) and natural stone (which needs periodic resealing) are both options depending on the finish you're after. Floor tiles within the shower zone use a slip-rated finish rather than the same polished tile used elsewhere in the room, since a wet shower floor and a dry hallway floor have different practical requirements even if you'd like them to visually match.

Site protection and clean-up

Dust sheeting and door seals go up before removal starts and stay up through the noisier, dustier stages, and the work area is swept and tidied at the end of each working day rather than left as a building site for the run of the job. Given how much shorter a shower conversion is than a full renovation, site protection matters just as much over those few days — a rushed job in week one is still a mess in someone's home. At completion, all debris, packaging and offcuts are removed from site, and we walk the finished shower with you before calling the job done.

Regional guide figures for the Algarve — your written quote will reflect your actual layout, fittings and property type. The single biggest driver of cost is whether the footprint changes: keeping the shower on the existing waste and supply points, in the same position the bath or old cubicle occupied, is consistently the cheapest and fastest route, while moving the drain point to a different wall adds pipework and, in an apartment, can mean working near a shared riser.

Beyond footprint, three choices move the price within any given scope: the base (a low-threshold tray costs less and fits faster than a tiled-in level-entry base, which needs the floor built up and gently falled to a drain), the screen (a framed or semi-framed screen is more affordable than a bespoke frameless panel, and a fixed panel is cheaper than a sliding or pivot door), and the tile format (standard porcelain sits at the lower end, while large-format porcelain or natural stone — which also needs periodic resealing — sit higher). Property access plays a role too: a ground-floor villa with a driveway is a simpler delivery and removal job than a fourth-floor apartment with a small lift, and that shows up in labour time rather than materials.

Wet Room vs. Walk-In Shower

How a walk-in shower conversion compares to a full wet room

The two are often confused, and it's a fair mix-up — both can end up looking similar from the doorway: glass, tiling, and a walk-in area with no bath. The difference is in what's happening underneath and around the walls, and that difference is what drives most of the gap in cost, floor work and maintenance between them.

Walk-In Shower ConversionWet Room Installation
Waterproofing scopeTanks and falls a defined shower zone — the tray or level-entry base area, plus walls to shower-head height around itTanks and falls the entire room floor and walls to a single drain point, so the whole room is safely wet-rated
Floor structureWorks within the existing floor build-up in most cases; a tiled-in base adds a shallow fall without rebuilding the whole floorUsually needs the floor built up, or in some cases dropped, to create a fall across the full room to one drain — more structural work
Typical cost€3,200 – €7,500€12,000 – €22,000+
MaintenanceA screen to keep clear of limescale, and a tray or kerb seam to check periodicallyA continuous tiled floor with no screen track or tray seam to maintain
Best suited toSmaller bathrooms, since only a defined zone needs tanking and fallingLarger bathrooms, with enough floor area to distribute a believable, gentle fall across the whole room

For resale, both read well to a buyer, but they read differently. A walk-in shower conversion signals a modernised, well-kept bathroom without implying anything unusual about the room's construction — it's a familiar fitting most buyers recognise immediately. A wet room reads as a more deliberate, higher-spec choice, and tends to appeal most strongly to buyers already thinking about long-term accessibility or a genuinely minimal, hotel-style finish; it can occasionally read as a strong personal preference to a buyer who'd have chosen a tray-based shower themselves, which is worth bearing in mind if resale within the next few years is a factor.

Room size matters more than most people expect. A wet room's floor fall needs to travel far enough across the room to be genuinely unnoticeable underfoot — cram that fall into a small bathroom and it either doesn't fall enough to drain properly, or falls so steeply over a short distance that you can feel it. A walk-in shower conversion sidesteps that problem entirely by only tanking and falling the shower zone itself, which is why it's usually the more practical route in a smaller bathroom, an ensuite, or a guest bathroom, regardless of budget.

If you're set on the full-room, trayless version, our wet room installation page covers that in full, including the same cost and process detail from the wet-room side. If a defined, screened shower zone within your existing room works for what you need, the guidance on this page already covers it.

Walk-In Shower vs. Like-For-Like Replacement

Is a walk-in conversion worth it, or should you just replace what's there?

If your existing shower tray and enclosure are sound and simply dated, a like-for-like swap — new tray, new screen, re-tiled — is usually the cheaper and faster route, and sits at the lower end of the ranges above. A walk-in conversion is worth the extra cost when the current tray has a step you want gone, when the enclosure is genuinely failing (cracked seals, persistent leaks at the tray edge), or when you're converting a bath that's no longer used into shower space. Neither option re-falls the whole room's floor the way a wet room does — see our full wet room vs. walk-in shower comparison if you're weighing that bigger step instead.
