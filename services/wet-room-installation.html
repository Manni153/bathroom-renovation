Who This Is For

Five situations that usually bring people to a wet room

Planning for long-term accessibility

No step, no tray lip, no threshold to catch a foot or a wheel — a wet room is the most robust way to future-proof a bathroom for ageing in place, without it needing to look institutional. This is often planned well ahead of any actual change in mobility, while the work can still be done calmly rather than as an emergency adaptation. What tends to worry people considering this route is that an accessible bathroom will end up looking clinical — grab rails, a plastic seat, visible drainage — but a properly tiled, level wet room with a well-chosen drain cover and fittings reads as a considered, contemporary bathroom first and an accessible one second. Once it's finished, the room works the same way for every visitor and every stage of life, which is really the point of doing it now rather than later.

Wanting a completely level, minimalist room

Some homeowners simply want the shower-anywhere look — a single continuous floor, no glass box in the middle of the room, no visible transition between "shower area" and "bathroom". This is usually a design-led decision rather than a practical necessity: the existing shower works fine, but the owner has seen the look in a villa or hotel and wants the same uninterrupted floor plane at home. The main question we get here is whether water ends up everywhere without a screen — with a properly formed fall and a sensibly positioned shower head it stays contained to the wet zone in normal use, though we're honest that a fully open layout suits confident, deliberate showering more than a household with young children splashing about. For the right room and household, it's the cleanest bathroom aesthetic available.

A ground-floor step-down threshold

Older Algarve properties sometimes have a stepped-down or sunken bathroom floor, a leftover of how the plumbing and drainage were originally run before modern falls and membranes were standard practice. Owners living with this day to day are usually more bothered by the trip hazard at the threshold than by anything cosmetic, and a like-for-like re-tile would simply rebuild the same step back in. Forming a wet room floor with a proper fall to drain can solve the step-down and the waterproofing in one job, because the floor is being rebuilt from the structure up rather than resurfaced over the existing problem. It's one of the more satisfying jobs for exactly that reason — a genuine, permanent fix rather than a repeat of the same compromise.

A villa bathroom being built from scratch

When a villa bathroom is being built from scratch as part of a wider renovation or extension, the floor structure is already open before any bathroom-specific work starts — slabs are exposed and levels are still being set, so nothing has to be undone to get at the sub-floor. In that situation, forming a proper fall to drain across the whole room costs comparatively little extra over a standard floor build-up, because the extra work is mostly a change in how the already-open floor is finished, rather than a separate demolition-and-rebuild exercise. This is often the most cost-effective point at which to specify a wet room, and one we flag proactively if we're already involved in a broader renovation. Owners in this position get the full wet room outcome without paying the full retrofit premium.

Water pooling or slow drainage in an existing shower

An owner who's noticed water pooling in a corner of the shower tray, slow drainage that takes longer each year, or a faint musty smell that keeps returning after cleaning is often looking at a tray that was never quite level to begin with, or has settled slightly over time. A tray replacement can fix the symptom temporarily, but it's addressing a symptom of an installation that was never properly engineered to fall correctly. Replacing the tray with a whole-room, professionally formed fall to a drain resolves the actual cause, and removes the tray-to-floor seam that's usually where the original problem started leaking or trapping water in the first place. It's a bigger job than swapping a tray, but for a room that's already shown you it has a drainage problem, it's the version that doesn't come back.

Wet room vs. walk-in shower — which is right for you?

Both give you a step-free shower. They differ in how much of the room is rebuilt to get there, and that difference shows up in cost, timeline and what's involved on-site. This page owns the full comparison — our walk-in shower conversion page links back here for it.

Honest summary: a wet room is a bigger structural undertaking than a walk-in shower conversion. If a level-access shower is the goal and your floor build-up or upper-floor apartment makes a full wet room impractical, a walk-in shower conversion may get you most of the day-to-day benefit for less cost and disruption — we'll say so on the site visit rather than defaulting to the bigger job.

A few practical differences are worth spelling out beyond the table. Day to day, a wet room's continuous tanked floor has no tray-to-wall seam and no door track or screen channel to collect grime and limescale, so cleaning tends to be a simple mop of one continuous surface. A walk-in shower's glass screen and tray edge, by contrast, need more regular cleaning around the frame and hinge, and the silicone seal where the screen meets the tray or wall typically needs resealing every few years to stay watertight.

From a resale perspective, a wet room reads to most buyers and valuers as a premium, deliberately considered renovation — but it's also a bigger structural claim about the property than a walk-in shower conversion, and not every buyer is looking for a fully screen-free bathroom. If you're renovating specifically ahead of a sale and don't know your buyer, a walk-in shower conversion is often the safer, more universally-liked choice; a wet room suits a home you plan to live in and enjoy for years more than one going straight to market.

Room size matters too, more than people expect going in. A wet room needs enough floor area to spread a believable fall to the drain without the gradient becoming steep and obvious right by the door — cram a whole-room fall into a genuinely small bathroom and either the fall has to be too aggressive near the threshold, or the drain ends up sitting awkwardly close to a wall. A walk-in shower conversion doesn't have this constraint, because only the shower's own footprint needs the fall, which is why it tends to be the more sensible choice in a small or standard-sized family bathroom.

Put simply: if your bathroom is a reasonable size, you're renovating for the long term, and a fully level, screen-optional room is genuinely what you want, a wet room is worth the extra cost and structural work. If your bathroom is compact, you want to keep costs and disruption down, or you're weighing resale appeal to an unknown future buyer, a walk-in shower conversion will very often get you most of the everyday benefit for meaningfully less. We'll give you a straight opinion on which fits your specific room during the site visit, rather than defaulting to the bigger job.

What's Included

Everything in a wet room installation

A wet room is delivered by our team as one continuous project. Here's what actually happens at each stage, and the practical questions that tend to come up along the way.

Strip-out and floor preparation

The room is stripped back to its shell, and because a wet room's floor is being rebuilt rather than resurfaced, this stage goes further than in a standard renovation — old tiling, the existing tray or bath, the sub-floor finish, and in most cases a section of the floor build-up itself all come out. We check the exposed slab or floor structure at this point against what was assumed at quote stage, since this is genuinely the moment that confirms whether recessing the slab or raising the build-up is the right route for your specific room. Everything removed is protected on its way out and disposed of through licensed waste channels, and the route through the property is sheeted and boarded before anything is carried through it.

Forming the falls

This is the step that defines a wet room: the whole bathroom floor, not just a shower footprint, is built or shaped to fall gently and consistently toward the drain from every direction. Depending on what the floor structure allows, this means either recessing the existing slab before rebuilding the floor on top, or building the floor up around a pre-formed drainage former designed to give an even fall without needing a deep recess. Getting the gradient right is a balance — steep enough that water actually clears rather than sitting, gentle enough that it isn't noticeable underfoot or awkward near the doorway. We set this out and check it before a single tile goes down, because a fall that's wrong at this stage can't be corrected later without lifting the floor again.

Whole-room tanking

Once the falls are formed, the entire floor and the lower section of every wall in the room — not just the shower zone — is waterproofed with a liquid-applied membrane system to NP EN 14891 practice. A wet room floor sees standing water across its whole surface during normal use, so the tanking has to be genuinely continuous, with no gaps at wall junctions, pipe penetrations or the drain collar where a smaller shower conversion might get away with a narrower wet zone. We give the membrane the cure time the manufacturer specifies between coats and before tiling starts, since this stage decides whether the room stays dry for fifteen years or develops a hidden leak into the floor below within a few.

Drain installation and detailing

The drain itself — a linear channel or a point drain, decided with you based on the room's layout and the direction of the fall — is set into the formed floor and tied into the tanking membrane with a purpose-made collar, since this junction is one of the most common places a wet room membrane fails if it's rushed. We check the drain runs freely and the fall clears water evenly across the whole floor before tiling begins, rather than assuming it's correct and finding out after the room is finished. Getting this stage right is the difference between a wet room that genuinely self-drains and one that leaves a damp patch by the door.

Tiling to the falls

Floor tiles are set to follow the formed falls exactly, with consistent grout lines maintained even as the floor gradient changes across the room — a genuinely more demanding cutting and setting job than tiling a flat floor, since every tile near the drain is subtly different from the ones further out. Wall tiling follows the same waterproof-compatible adhesive system used across the rest of the room. Most clients choose porcelain for its durability, low maintenance and slip resistance underfoot when wet, though large-format porcelain and natural stone are both options depending on the look you want — we talk through the practical trade-offs, including how each performs on a sloped, always-wet floor, rather than assuming one default.

The screen or open layout

A wet room can be finished with a glass screen or partition to contain spray to one part of the room, or left completely open with no barrier at all — both are genuine options, not one being a compromise version of the other. An open layout gives the cleanest look and the easiest room to clean, but suits confident, deliberate showering more than a household where water is more likely to end up further from the drain. A screen keeps spray more contained without reintroducing a step or a tray, since it sits on the same continuous, formed floor as the rest of the room. We talk through which suits your household during the site visit rather than defaulting to either option.

Fixtures

New fixtures — the shower valve and head, the drain cover finish, a vanity unit, taps and sanitaryware — are supplied and fitted to your chosen specification, or to items you've sourced yourself if you'd rather buy directly. The shower valve position and head type matter more in a wet room than a boxed-in shower, since without a screen (or with only a partial one) spray pattern affects how contained the water stays to the wet zone. We talk through this properly at quote stage — a fixed overhead rainfall head throws water differently to a handheld or wall-mounted mixer, and the right choice depends on your room's layout and fall direction, not just the look you like in a showroom.

Site protection and clean-up

Dust sheeting and door seals stay up for the duration of the noisier, dustier stages — strip-out and floor-forming work in particular, since a wet room's floor work runs deeper and longer than a standard renovation's — and the work area is swept and tidied at the end of each working day rather than left as a building site until completion. At the end of the job, all debris, packaging and any leftover floor build-up material is removed from site, and we walk the finished room with you, checking the fall drains properly and the finish is right, before calling it complete.

Cost Guidance

What a wet room installation typically costs

The single biggest cost driver is whether the floor structure needs altering to form the fall to drain. Ground-floor and villa wet rooms are usually more straightforward — and cheaper — than upper-floor apartment conversions, where slab depth and the flat below are a real constraint.

ScenarioTypical rangeTypical duration
Ground floor / villa, slab can be recessed€12,000 – €16,50012–15 working days
Upper-floor apartment, floor build-up raised€16,500 – €22,000+15–18 working days

Upper-floor figures assume the floor build-up can be raised without exceeding what the door threshold and ceiling height below can absorb — confirmed on-site, and coordinated with your condomínio where the works affect shared structure. Renovation labour on residential properties over two years old may also qualify for Portugal's reduced 6% IVA rate instead of the standard 23% — we confirm eligibility as part of your quote.

Beyond the floor structure question, three other factors move the price within either scenario. Drain type is one: a linear channel drain generally costs more than a simple point drain, both in the component itself and in the added precision needed to form a straight, consistent fall to a channel rather than to a single point — though a linear drain often works better in a larger or oddly-shaped room. Room size and tiling complexity is another — a bigger floor means more tiles cut to a changing gradient, which is slower and more skilled work than tiling a flat floor of the same area. And whether you choose a full glass screen, a partial one, or a fully open layout has its own cost implications, both in the screen itself and in how much margin the fall and tiling need around the wet zone. We walk through all three with you at quote stage so the final figure reflects real decisions, not just a size-based estimate.
