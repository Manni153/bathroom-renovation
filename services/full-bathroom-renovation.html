Who This Is For

Five situations that usually bring people to us

The bathroom feels dated

1990s or 2000s tiling in a colour nobody would choose today, a bathtub that hasn't been used in years, taps that have gone dull, and lighting that makes the whole room feel dim rather than clean. None of it is broken exactly — it's just tired, and a patch repair here and there never quite fixes the feeling. What usually brings people to a full renovation rather than a partial update is realising that fixing one thing (the tiles, say) would leave the rest looking even more dated by comparison, so it makes more sense to do the whole room properly, once, and be done with it for another fifteen to twenty years.

Renovating before selling

An outdated bathroom is one of the first things a buyer or a valuer notices on a viewing, and it's an easy detail for them to use as a negotiating point even when the rest of the property is in good order. A full renovation with neutral, well-finished materials — porcelain tiling, a clean modern suite, nothing too personal or trend-led — protects your asking price and removes an objection before it's raised. We hear from a number of owners who are timing this specifically around a planned sale, and we plan the schedule around that date rather than working to a vague "sometime before we list it" timeline.

Converting for accessibility

Replacing a step-in bath or a shower tray with a lip for a level-access layout, usually for long-term comfort as mobility changes, or because a family member visiting regularly finds the current setup difficult. This can be done as part of a full renovation within the existing footprint, or taken further into a fully tanked, kerb-free wet room — see our wet room installation page for that version, and our comparison of the two approaches further down this page if you're not yet sure which fits your bathroom.

Consolidating years of small fixes into one job

A replaced tap here, a re-grout there, a shower door that was swapped out three years ago — bathrooms often end up as a patchwork of small, separately-timed repairs rather than one coherent room. Individually none of those fixes was wrong, but together they rarely look intentional, and repairing around old tiling or an ageing membrane usually costs more over time than one properly planned renovation would have. This is a common route for owners who've been in the property a decade or more and are ready to stop patching.

Water damage or a failed membrane forcing the issue

Sometimes the decision isn't really a choice — a leak has already damaged a wall, tiles have come loose because the adhesive or membrane beneath them failed, or a ceiling stain in the room below has made it clear that water has been getting through for a while. In these cases a full renovation is often the more sensible route rather than a spot repair, since it lets us properly waterproof the whole wet area to a current standard rather than patching around an unknown problem and risking the same failure recurring in another spot a year later.

What's Included

Everything between strip-out and final fix

A full renovation is delivered by our team as one continuous project. Here's what actually happens at each stage, and the practical questions that tend to come up along the way.

Strip-out and clearance

The room is stripped back to its shell — old tiling, the existing suite, any old flooring, and anything else being replaced comes out first. We protect the route from the bathroom to the front door or lift with sheeting and boards before anything is carried through the property, and everything removed is bagged or boxed for disposal through licensed waste channels rather than left in a skip on the street for weeks. If there's a fixture in good condition you'd rather keep, sell on, or donate instead of having it disposed of, we set it aside if you tell us in advance.

Waterproofing and tanking

Once the room is back to bare walls and floor, every wet zone — the shower area, the floor, and the lower section of walls in direct-splash zones — is waterproofed with a liquid-applied membrane system to NP EN 14891 practice. This is the stage that decides whether the room lasts fifteen years or develops a hidden leak in three, so we take the cure time the membrane manufacturer specifies between coats and before tiling seriously, rather than compressing it to hit a deadline. Floor-drain collars, internal corners and the wall-floor junction get particular attention, since those are the points where a membrane typically fails first if it's rushed or poorly detailed.

Tiling

The floor is levelled where needed — older Algarve properties in particular often have floors that aren't quite flat, and a self-levelling compound or a properly bedded screed sorts that out before a single tile goes down. From there, tiles are set on a waterproof-compatible adhesive, with attention to consistent grout lines and correct falls to the drain in shower areas. Most clients choose porcelain for the durability and low maintenance, though large-format porcelain and natural stone (which needs periodic resealing) are both options depending on the look you're after — we go through this at quote stage rather than defaulting to one option.

Sanitaryware and fixtures

New sanitaryware — WC, basin, shower enclosure or bath, vanity unit, and taps — is supplied and fitted to your chosen specification, or to items you've sourced yourself if you'd rather buy directly. This is usually where the room's character comes from: a freestanding bath versus a built-in one, a wall-hung vanity versus a floor-standing unit, a rainfall shower head versus a standard mixer. We talk through the practical trade-offs of each (a wall-hung vanity is easier to clean under but needs a stud wall or bracket strong enough to take the weight, for example) rather than just fitting whatever's on the moodboard.

Plastering and finishing

Any wall or ceiling surface disturbed by the strip-out or by running new pipework is plastered and made good before painting, so the finished room doesn't have a visible seam where old meets new. Skirting, architrave and any trim are reinstated or replaced to match the new floor level and finish, and paint is applied to a standard that holds up to bathroom humidity rather than a general-purpose interior finish that will bubble within a year.

Site protection and clean-up

Dust sheeting and door seals stay up for the duration of the noisier, dustier stages (strip-out and tile-cutting in particular), and the work area is swept and tidied at the end of each day rather than left as a building site until completion. At the end of the job, all debris and packaging is removed from site, and we walk the room with you before calling it finished.

Cost Guidance

What a full renovation typically costs

Regional guide figures for the Algarve — your written quote will reflect your actual layout, finishes and property type. The single biggest driver of cost within any renovation is whether the layout changes: keeping fixtures on their existing waste and supply runs is consistently the cheapest and fastest route, while moving a shower or adding a second basin means new pipework and, sometimes, permit considerations.

Renovation labour on residential properties over two years old may qualify for Portugal's reduced 6% IVA rate instead of the standard 23% — we confirm eligibility as part of your quote.

Beyond layout, the other real cost drivers are finish level (porcelain versus natural stone, a standard mixer versus a thermostatic shower valve, a stock vanity versus a custom joinery piece) and property access — a villa with a driveway is a simpler logistical job than a fourth-floor apartment with a small lift and a shared entrance, and that difference shows up in labour time rather than materials cost.

A Common Question

Full renovation, or a smaller update?

Not every tired bathroom needs a full renovation. If the layout works, the waterproofing is sound, and it's really the surface finish that's let the room down, our bathroom tiling page covers a re-tile on its own, and our plumbing & fixtures page covers swapping taps, a shower valve, or sanitaryware without touching the rest of the room. A full renovation makes more sense once more than one of those elements needs attention at the same time, when the existing waterproofing is unknown or suspect, or when you're changing the layout — at that point, doing everything as one coordinated project is usually both cheaper and less disruptive than a series of separate jobs spread over a year or two. We'll tell you plainly during the site visit which category your bathroom falls into, rather than defaulting to the larger scope.
