Who This Is For

Five situations that usually bring people to us

Grout has gone black or is falling out

Grout that's cracked, crumbling, or gone dark with mould despite regular cleaning is a genuine giveaway — not scare talk — that the waterproofing membrane beneath is likely compromised too. It's easy to treat this as a cosmetic problem and simply re-grout over it, but if water is already getting past the tile joints, a fresh line of grout only hides the entry point rather than closing it. We check the substrate underneath before recommending anything, and if the membrane has failed, re-tiling with a fresh NP EN 14891 waterproofing layer beneath is the only way to actually stop water getting into the wall or floor structure, rather than just papering over where it's getting in. Catching this early is usually far cheaper than waiting until the damage shows up as damp in an adjoining room.

Wanting a more contemporary look

Small, busy tile patterns from a previous decade — a mix of colours, a heavy border tile, a glossy 1990s finish — are one of the most common reasons owners come to us, usually wanting large-format porcelain or natural stone for a cleaner, more current finish. This is generally the most straightforward kind of tiling job, because it doesn't necessarily touch the layout, plumbing or fixtures — the room's footprint stays the same and only the surface changes. What does change with a move to large-format tiling is the substrate requirement: fewer, bigger tiles show up an uneven wall or floor far more obviously than a small mosaic ever would, so the prep work usually matters more than owners expect going in. We talk through what a genuinely flat result requires during the site visit, rather than quoting a fast job that shows lippage six months later.

Tiling on its own, or inside a bigger job

This page covers tiling as a standalone job — a re-tile where the rest of the bathroom (the suite, the plumbing, the layout) is staying as it is. If you're also changing fixtures, moving the shower, or replacing sanitaryware at the same time, our full bathroom renovation page covers that instead, and tiling is scheduled inside that larger project rather than quoted separately. The distinction matters for cost and timing more than most people expect: a standalone re-tile that keeps everything else in place is usually faster and considerably cheaper than doing the same tiling work as an add-on partway through a renovation, because the room doesn't need protecting and re-protecting twice. If you're not sure which category your bathroom falls into, our comparison further down this page covers it in more detail.

Matching or extending existing tiling

Sometimes it's not a full re-tile at all — a section of wall came down during a repair, a single cracked floor tile needs replacing, or a small extension to an existing run is needed after adding a shelf or a niche, and the original tile has since been discontinued by the manufacturer. This is a genuinely fiddly job: an exact colour and shade match is rarely possible once a tile range has been out of production for a few years, even with the same reference code, because manufacturing batches vary over time. We're upfront about this before starting — sometimes the better result is a deliberate contrast strip or border rather than a match that will always look slightly off under close inspection, and we'll talk through the realistic options rather than promise an invisible repair we can't actually deliver.

Wanting a specific decorative look

A herringbone floor pattern, a mosaic feature strip as a shower-niche accent, or book-matched marble-effect porcelain slabs laid to mirror each other across a wall — these looks are increasingly requested, and they need considerably more careful setting-out than a standard grid layout. Pattern tiling has less room for error: a herringbone run that starts from the wrong point in the room draws the eye to an awkward cut at the far wall, and book-matching only works if the slabs are ordered, numbered and laid in the sequence they were cut. We plan the setting-out against the actual room dimensions before cutting a single tile, and it's worth allowing more time and a slightly higher labour cost for this kind of work compared with a standard grid — we'll be upfront about that difference at quote stage rather than let it come as a surprise once the job is under way.

What's Included

What actually happens between strip-out and grout

Tiling that lasts is mostly decided before the tiling starts. Here's what actually happens at each stage, and the practical questions that tend to come up along the way.

Substrate preparation and levelling

Before any tile is set, the substrate — the floor screed or wall surface underneath — needs to be sound, clean and reasonably flat. Older Algarve properties in particular often have floors that aren't quite level or walls with an uneven plaster finish, and rather than tiling over that and hoping it doesn't show, we address it directly: a self-levelling compound on floors, or a levelling render coat on walls where needed. This step matters more than it might seem — an unlevel substrate under large-format tiles shows up as lippage (edges that sit slightly proud of each other) that's both visible and a genuine trip and chip risk, so we budget the prep time properly rather than rush a tile down on a surface that isn't ready for it.

Waterproofing membrane beneath the tiles

Every wet zone — the shower area, the floor, and the lower section of walls in direct-splash areas — gets a liquid-applied waterproofing membrane compliant with NP EN 14891 before any tile is set, whether the job is a standalone re-tile or part of a wider renovation. This is the layer that actually keeps water out of the wall and floor structure; the tiles and grout on top are attractive but not, on their own, watertight. We pay particular attention to the floor-drain collar, internal corners and the wall-floor junction, since these are the points where a membrane typically fails first if it's applied without enough care, and we give the membrane the cure time the manufacturer specifies before tiling starts rather than compressing it to save a day.

Tile-setting and adhesive selection

The adhesive used to fix the tile is matched to what's being tiled, not applied as one default mix for every job. Porcelain on a stable floor generally needs a standard cementitious adhesive; large-format tiles need a high-strength, low-slump adhesive applied with a specific notched trowel to avoid voids behind the tile that can later crack under weight; and natural stone needs a white, non-staining adhesive so moisture in the mix doesn't discolour the stone from behind, which is a real risk with the wrong product. Corners and returns are cut and fitted individually rather than filled with offcuts, and where a run has to end at an internal corner or against a fixture, we plan the layout so the cut pieces land somewhere unobtrusive rather than as a narrow sliver at eye level.

Grout and sealant

Grout colour and type is chosen to suit the tile and the room — a contrasting grout line reads very differently to a colour-matched one, and epoxy grout, while more expensive and less forgiving to apply, resists staining and cracking better than standard cementitious grout on a heavily used shower floor. Anywhere two different surfaces meet, or wherever the structure can move slightly (the joint between floor and wall, around the shower tray, along a bath edge), we use a flexible silicone sealant rather than grout, because grout is rigid and will crack at exactly those movement points over time. Sealant colour is matched to the grout so the finish reads as one continuous line rather than a visible seam.

Finishing and edge trims

Tile edges — around a niche, at an external corner, or where tiling meets a different material like a wooden floor or a painted wall — are finished with a proper edge trim rather than left as a raw, easily-chipped cut edge. The choice of trim (metal, PVC, or a mitred edge on the tile itself) depends on the look you're after and the tile thickness, and we go through the options rather than defaulting to whichever is fastest to fit. Once grouting and sealing are done, the room needs roughly 24–48 hours before it can be used normally, and longer than that — usually 5–7 days — before the grout has fully cured to handle a full daily shower routine without staining.

How It Works

From first call to final sign-off

Site visit

We inspect the existing tiling, check what condition the substrate and any existing waterproofing are likely to be in, and measure the area to be covered. For a straightforward re-tile this is usually enough to quote from directly.

Written, itemised quote

A fixed price for the tile area, material choice and prep work involved, with a realistic timeline — not a rough verbal figure. You take the time you need to review it before committing to anything.

Scheduling

We confirm a start date, order the tile and adhesive with enough lead time to avoid delays mid-job, and agree access arrangements — including with a keyholder if you're not on-site for the work.

Strip-out of old tiles

Old tiles, grout and any failed waterproofing are removed back to the substrate. This is also when we get a proper look at what's actually underneath — not just what the site visit could see from the surface.

Substrate prep and waterproofing

Any levelling or repair the substrate needs is carried out, then the wet zones are waterproofed to NP EN 14891 practice and given the cure time the membrane needs before tiling starts.

Tiling and grouting

Tiles are set, cut and finished around fixtures and corners, then grouted and sealed. Photo updates are sent at each major stage if you're not on-site to see it in person.

Final walkthrough

A final inspection with you — in person or by video call — before we call the job finished. Any snag items are agreed and closed out before final payment.

Tile Options

What's commonly used in Algarve bathrooms

  • Standard porcelain — the most common and cost-effective choice, durable and low-maintenance, in a wide range of finishes
  • Large-format porcelain (roughly 60x120cm and up) — a cleaner, more contemporary look with fewer grout lines, but it needs a flatter substrate and a specific high-strength adhesive
  • Natural stone — limestone or marble for a distinct, high-end finish; porous, so it needs sealing at installation and periodic resealing afterwards
  • Mosaic — commonly used for shower floors, where small tiles allow the fall needed for drainage, and as feature strips or borders

A Common Question

Is a re-tile enough, or does the bathroom need a full renovation?

A re-tile on its own genuinely solves the problem when the layout works, the fixtures are in good condition, and what's actually let the room down is the surface finish or the waterproofing beneath it — tired tiling, cracked grout, or a membrane that's failed but hasn't yet damaged anything beyond the tiled area itself. In that situation, tiling as a standalone job (with proper waterproofing underneath, which we treat as non-negotiable regardless of scope) gets the room back to a sound, current-looking state without the cost or disruption of touching plumbing or sanitaryware that doesn't need it. See our waterproofing page for more on what a compromised membrane actually means for the structure behind the tiles.

It becomes a false economy compared with a full renovation in a couple of specific situations: if water has already got past a failed membrane and affected areas beyond what a re-tile would touch — a damp patch on an adjoining wall, staining in the ceiling below, or damage to the floor structure itself — then re-tiling alone treats the symptom without addressing what's already happened elsewhere in the room. It's also often not the most efficient route if the fixtures are separately due for replacement anyway; doing a re-tile now and a fixture swap in a year or two usually costs more in total than doing both as one project, because the room gets protected, disrupted and finished twice instead of once. Our full bathroom renovation page covers what that combined project looks like, and during the site visit we'll tell you plainly which category your bathroom falls into rather than upsell the larger scope by default.
