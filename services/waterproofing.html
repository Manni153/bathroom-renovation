Who This Is For

Five situations that usually bring people to this page

A stain on the ceiling below

A damp patch, discolouration or a ring of bubbling paint shows up on the ceiling of the room below a bathroom — sometimes noticed by the owner, sometimes flagged by a downstairs neighbour or a tenant. It's tempting to treat it as a ceiling problem and simply repaint over it, but the water is coming from somewhere above, and painting over the symptom does nothing to stop the source. In a building with a condomínio, this is also a shared-liability question that tends to get more complicated the longer it's left, since it becomes harder to establish when the leak started and whose responsibility the damage is. We open up the bathroom above to find where the water is actually getting through — usually a specific failure point rather than the whole membrane — fix it properly, and give you a written record of what was found, which matters if insurance or the condomínio administration gets involved.

Re-tiling reveals a problem

Tiles come up for what was meant to be a straightforward re-tiling job, and the waterproofing underneath turns out to be cracked, patchy, or — in a good number of older Algarve properties — simply never installed, with tiles laid directly onto a cement screed or the bare substrate. This is one of the most common discoveries we make once a floor or shower wall is opened up, and it's rarely visible from the surface beforehand; old tiling can look completely sound right up until it's removed. When it happens mid-job, we stop, show you what's underneath, and give you a written price for bringing it up to a proper waterproofed standard rather than re-tiling over the same unprotected substrate and letting the same problem resurface in a few years. Most people in this position choose to have it done properly at that point, since the tiles are already up and the extra cost is far lower than reopening the room again later.

Building a wet room, once, properly

Villa owners building a wet room, or a bathroom against an exterior wall, who want the tanking done right the first time rather than finding out five years later that it wasn't. This group has often done some research already — they've heard of NP EN 14891, they know to ask about upstand height and cure time, and they're frequently choosing a wet room specifically because its kerb-free, walk-in design puts more of the room under sustained water exposure than a conventional enclosure does. For a new wet room, that raised exposure is exactly why the waterproofing detailing has to be treated as the most important stage of the whole job, not a step to move past quickly — a mistake here doesn't show up on day one, it shows up as a ceiling stain or a soft floor a few years into ownership.

Assessing a resale property before committing to a budget

Buyers about to commit to a renovation budget on a resale property who want the existing bathroom's waterproofing independently assessed first, before finishes and layout decisions get locked in. This is a common step for anyone buying an older Algarve property, particularly where the current bathroom looks fine on the surface but its age or the building's history makes it worth checking before assuming it's sound. We can open up a discreet section of tiling, or in some cases give an honest read from visible signs alone, on whether the existing membrane is salvageable, needs partial repair, or should be budgeted for as absent — information that belongs in your renovation figures from the start rather than surfacing as a surprise once work is already underway.

A shower that's been leaking slowly for a while

A shower that's been leaking slowly around the tray edge or the screen seal for a while, often for long enough that nobody quite remembers when it started — grout darkening a little, a slightly soft feel underfoot, nothing dramatic enough to investigate on its own. The damage usually only becomes obvious once it's tracked to its source: a swollen skirting board, a damp patch that keeps returning after redecorating, or a ceiling stain below that finally prompts someone to look properly. Because the leak has been slow rather than sudden, water has often been getting into the substrate for months, so the repair usually needs to address more than the seal itself — we assess how far the moisture has actually spread before deciding how much of the surrounding area needs to be opened up and re-waterproofed, rather than just resealing the visible gap and leaving a compromised substrate underneath.

What's Actually Involved

What proper waterproofing means, stage by stage

Waterproofing is a system, not a single coat of paint-on membrane. Most failures we see trace back to the details, not the product — here's what actually happens at each stage.

Assessing existing conditions

Once tiles are off, or where we're assessing before work is even underway, the first job is establishing what's actually there. Older Algarve properties in particular can turn out to have no membrane at all — tiles bedded straight onto a cement screed — or a membrane that was applied once but never dressed up the walls or across the drain collar properly. We look for cracking, delamination where the membrane has lifted from the substrate, degraded product past its useful life, and dampness in the screed itself that indicates water has already been getting through for some time. Where we can't fully assess without opening up more of the room, we say so honestly rather than guessing, since covering a suspect substrate back up with new tiling just resets the same problem on a delay.

Membrane selection and application

We use liquid-applied membrane systems compliant with NP EN 14891, chosen for the specific substrate and area rather than defaulting to one product everywhere — a shower floor with a drain and falls has different requirements to a wall in a splash zone. The membrane goes on in multiple coats, typically two, each applied once the previous coat has properly set, since a single thick coat is more prone to pinholing and uneven cure than two thinner ones. Just as important as the membrane itself is confirming it's compatible with the tile adhesive going over it — some combinations don't bond properly to each other even when each product is individually sound, which can cause tiles to debond months later for a reason that has nothing to do with the membrane's waterproofing performance.

Detailing at failure points

Flat, open areas of floor or wall are the easiest part of a membrane to get right — it's the joints and transitions where real-world failures actually happen. The floor-drain collar needs a purpose-made sealing collar bonded into the membrane around the drain body, not just membrane painted up to the edge of a hole; that junction takes constant water flow and is one of the highest-risk points in the whole room. Internal corners — wall meeting wall, wall meeting floor — need reinforcing tape or fabric embedded into the membrane, because a membrane simply painted across a corner will crack at that fold as the building moves seasonally, however slightly. The wall-floor junction gets the same treatment for the same reason. None of this is visible once tiling goes over it, which is exactly why it has to be right the first time.

Upstand height and coverage zones

Walls in the direct shower zone are waterproofed up to roughly 1.8–2m — high enough to cover the area actually hit by moving water, including from a shower head positioned above head height or water splashing off a screen. Outside the direct spray zone, the same full-height treatment isn't needed; the membrane steps down to a lower splash-zone height on the rest of the bathroom walls, since treating the entire room to full height adds cost and time without adding meaningful protection where a wall is never actually wet. Getting this coverage map right — rather than under-protecting the shower zone or over-specifying the rest of the room — is decided during the site visit based on the actual layout, not applied as a blanket rule.

Cure time

This is the stage most likely to be rushed, and rushing it is the single most common cause of waterproofing failure we see in practice. A liquid-applied membrane needs 24–48 hours between coats to properly set before the next one goes on, and then a further cure period — commonly another 24–72 hours depending on the product, temperature and humidity — before it's ready to be tiled over. Tiling too early traps uncured membrane under a rigid, adhered surface, which can prevent it from ever curing correctly and creates a failure that won't show up for months or years, hidden under finished tiling by the time it does. We build cure time into the schedule from the outset rather than treating it as a delay to be absorbed if the job is running late, because it's the one stage where cutting corners doesn't save time overall — it just moves the cost of the mistake further down the line.

Honest Guidance

When old waterproofing has already failed

Waterproofing failure is rarely obvious from inside a finished bathroom. A membrane can be cracked, missing at a corner joint, or simply never installed, and the room can still look completely normal for years — until water finds its way through the substrate and shows up somewhere else, usually as a stain on a ceiling below.

Because of that, diagnosing a failed membrane usually isn't a job on its own. In most cases it's only confirmed once tiles are already coming up for a re-tiling or renovation project, or once damage in the room below has made it clear something needs opening up. We'll always tell you honestly what we find before covering it back up with new tiling — including if the honest answer is that the original waterproofing was never installed to any standard at all, which is common in older Algarve properties.

Tiling is reinstated and finished to match the surrounding area, the room is cleaned, and we walk through the completed work with you before calling the job finished. Any snag items are agreed and closed out before final payment.

Cost Guidance

What waterproofing typically costs

Waterproofing is priced differently depending on whether it's a standalone repair or part of a larger job. For remedial work, the single biggest driver of cost isn't the membrane material itself — it's how much of the room needs to be opened up to properly diagnose and fix the problem. A leak traced quickly to one drain collar or one corner joint might only need a small section of tiling removed and re-waterproofed; the same leak left for longer, or one that's tracked further through the substrate before it's investigated, can mean an entire wall or floor needs opening up, which changes both the price and the timeline substantially.

Waterproofing folded into a full renovation or re-tiling project doesn't carry these same variables in the same way, since the whole floor and wet-zone walls are being stripped back and waterproofed as standard regardless of prior condition.

Apartment Buildings

When a leak affects a shared building

A waterproofing failure in an apartment is rarely just your problem to solve quietly — if water has been tracking through to the floor below, it typically becomes a conversation with a neighbour, and sometimes with the condomínio administration, before it's a conversation about repair work. We can't tell you what your insurance policy covers, and we won't promise a particular outcome from a claim — that depends on your specific policy, the cause identified, and how the condomínio's own insurance interacts with yours, none of which we control.

What we can do is give you the kind of paper trail that actually helps when a claim or a neighbour dispute is being worked through: photographs of the affected area and the failure point before we touch anything, a written record of what was found and why, the specification of the membrane system used in the repair, and a signed-off scope of works once the job is complete. In our experience, disputes between neighbours over a shared-building leak are resolved faster and with less friction when there's a clear, dated record of what happened and what was done about it, rather than a verbal account after the fact. If your building's regulamento do condomínio requires notifying the administration before opening up shared risers or common walls, we're glad to provide a written scope of works you can pass along — this is worth doing early, before work starts, rather than as a report handed over afterward.
